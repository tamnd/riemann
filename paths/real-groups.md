This chapter gives the representation theory of real reductive groups that the archimedean places of the proof need: $(\mathfrak{g}, K)$-modules, the Casimir operator and infinitesimal characters, principal series, the asymptotics of matrix coefficients, discrete series and their formal degrees, tempered representations and the Langlands classification, and the unitary dual of $\mathrm{GL}_n$. The [unitary hypotheses](/proof/unitary-hypotheses/) take these facts as named inputs (`harishChandraModules`, `casselmanWallach`, `discreteSeriesCover`, `discreteSeriesClassification`, `harishChandraFinite`, `harishChandraAnalyticity`, `genericUnitaryDualGL`). The [Bessel seesaw](/proof/bessel-seesaw/) imposes interlacing on Harish-Chandra parameters of discrete series of $\mathrm{U}(p, q)$, [HypsAlt is nonempty](/step/nonempty-hyps/) uses the discrete series cover and the Casselman-Wallach theorem, and the development volumes prove pointwise bounds for discrete series matrix coefficients. We assume the chapter on [representations of local groups](/learn/representations/), whose sections on real groups and discrete series state the first definitions, and the chapter on [classical groups](/learn/groups/).

## Real reductive groups

Let $G \subseteq \mathrm{GL}_N(\mathbb{C})$ be the group of real points of a connected reductive group over $\mathbb{R}$, stable under $g \mapsto \bar{g}^T$, as in [reductive groups and maximal compact subgroups](/learn/groups/#reductive-groups-and-maximal-compact-subgroups). Let $\theta(g) = (\bar{g}^T)^{-1}$ be the Cartan involution and $K = G \cap \mathrm{U}(N)$ its fixed group, a [[maximal compact subgroup|T2EB8R9HEV]]. Let $\mathfrak{g}_0$ be the [[Lie algebra|5JJYBJ6A8K]] of $G$, $\mathfrak{g}_0 = \mathfrak{k}_0 \oplus \mathfrak{p}_0$ the eigenspace decomposition of $d\theta$ for the eigenvalues $1$ and $-1$, and $\mathfrak{g} = \mathfrak{g}_0 \otimes_\mathbb{R} \mathbb{C}$. The group $G$ can be disconnected, as $\mathrm{GL}_n(\mathbb{R})$ is, but by the [[Cartan decomposition|K7VVYFE9YJ]] $K$ meets each component.

**Definition (restricted roots).** Let $\mathfrak{a}_0 \subseteq \mathfrak{p}_0$ be a maximal abelian subspace; its dimension is the *real rank* of $G$. For $\alpha \in \mathfrak{a}_0^*$ put $\mathfrak{g}_\alpha = \{X \in \mathfrak{g}_0 : [H, X] = \alpha(H) X \text{ for all } H \in \mathfrak{a}_0\}$. The *restricted roots* are the $\alpha \ne 0$ with $\mathfrak{g}_\alpha \ne 0$, and $m_\alpha = \dim \mathfrak{g}_\alpha$ is the *multiplicity* of $\alpha$. Fix a positive system $\Sigma^+$ with simple roots $\Delta$, and let $\mathfrak{a}_0^+ = \{H : \alpha(H) > 0 \text{ for } \alpha \in \Sigma^+\}$ be the positive chamber. Put $\rho = \frac{1}{2} \sum_{\alpha \in \Sigma^+} m_\alpha \alpha$, $\mathfrak{n}_0 = \bigoplus_{\alpha \in \Sigma^+} \mathfrak{g}_\alpha$, $N_0 = \exp \mathfrak{n}_0$, $A_0 = \exp \mathfrak{a}_0$ and $M_0 = Z_K(\mathfrak{a}_0)$.

**Theorem (Iwasawa and $KAK$ decompositions).** (1) The map $(k, a, n) \mapsto kan$ is a diffeomorphism $K \times A_0 \times N_0 \to G$. (2) Each $g \in G$ is $k \exp(H) k'$ with $k, k' \in K$ and a unique $H \in \overline{\mathfrak{a}_0^+}$. (3) For a suitable normalization of the [[Haar measure|CE0V9686M8]] $dg$ and with $dk$ of total mass $1$,
$$\int_G f(g)\, dg = \int_K \int_{\mathfrak{a}_0^+} \int_K f(k \exp(H) k') \prod_{\alpha \in \Sigma^+} \sinh(\alpha(H))^{m_\alpha}\, dk\, dH\, dk'.$$

For (1) and (2) see Knapp, *Lie Groups Beyond an Introduction*, Chapters VI and VII; for (3) see Helgason, *Groups and Geometric Analysis*, Chapter I, §5. Part (1) is the [[Iwasawa decomposition|ZYVEVYBA41]]. Since $\sinh t \le e^t / 2$, the density in (3) is at most $e^{2\rho(H)}$. Away from the walls it is bounded below by a constant times $e^{2\rho(H)}$. So the growth of $G$ in the direction $H$ is $e^{2\rho(H)}$, and each $L^p$ estimate on $G$ below reduces to an integral over $\mathfrak{a}_0^+$ with this weight.

**Definition (parabolic subgroups).** The group $P_0 = M_0 A_0 N_0$ is the *minimal parabolic subgroup*. For $\Theta \subseteq \Delta$, let $\mathfrak{a} = \bigcap_{\alpha \in \Theta} \ker \alpha$, $A = \exp \mathfrak{a}$, and let $N$ be generated by the $\mathfrak{g}_\alpha$ with $\alpha > 0$ not in the span of $\Theta$. The group $P = Z_G(A) N$ is the *standard [[parabolic subgroup|Z2FEBW5MQM]]* attached to $\Theta$. Then $Z_G(A) = M \times A$ for a closed subgroup $M$, and $P = MAN$ is the *Langlands decomposition*. Put $\rho_P(H) = \frac{1}{2} \operatorname{tr}(\operatorname{ad} H|_{\mathfrak{n}_0 \cap \operatorname{Lie} N})$ for $H \in \mathfrak{a}$. The modular function of $P$ is $\delta_P(man) = e^{2\rho_P(\log a)}$.

The precise description of $M$ in the Langlands decomposition of parabolic subgroups is in Knapp, *Lie Groups Beyond an Introduction*, Chapter VII, in the section on parabolic subgroups. For $\mathrm{GL}_n$ it is explicit.

**Example ($\mathrm{GL}_n(\mathbb{R})$).** Here $K = \mathrm{O}(n)$, $\mathfrak{p}_0$ is the space of real symmetric matrices, and $\mathfrak{a}_0$ is the space of real diagonal matrices. The restricted roots are $e_i - e_j$, $i \ne j$, each of multiplicity $1$. With $\Sigma^+ = \{e_i - e_j : i < j\}$, $N_0$ is the group of upper triangular unipotent matrices, $M_0 = \{\pm 1\}^n$ is the group of diagonal sign matrices, and $\rho = (\frac{n-1}{2}, \frac{n-3}{2}, \dots, \frac{1-n}{2})$. The standard parabolic subgroups are the block upper triangular groups for $n = n_1 + \dots + n_r$. For such $P$, $M$ is the group of block diagonal $(g_1, \dots, g_r)$ with $|\det g_i| = 1$, and $A$ is the group of block scalars $(t_1 1_{n_1}, \dots, t_r 1_{n_r})$, $t_i > 0$.

**Example ($\mathrm{GL}_n(\mathbb{C})$).** Here $K = \mathrm{U}(n)$ and $\mathfrak{a}_0$ is again the real diagonal matrices. The roots are $e_i - e_j$, each of multiplicity $2$, $M_0 = \mathrm{U}(1)^n$, and $\rho = (n-1, n-3, \dots, 1-n)$.

**Example ($\mathrm{U}(p, q)$).** Let $p \ge q \ge 1$, $J = \operatorname{diag}(1_p, -1_q)$ and $G = \mathrm{U}(p, q) = \{g : \bar{g}^T J g = J\}$. Then $K = \mathrm{U}(p) \times \mathrm{U}(q)$, and the real rank is $q$. In suitable coordinates on $\mathfrak{a}_0 \cong \mathbb{R}^q$, the restricted roots are $\pm e_i \pm e_j$ ($i < j$) of multiplicity $2$, $\pm e_i$ of multiplicity $2(p - q)$, and $\pm 2e_i$ of multiplicity $1$. This is the root system $BC_q$; if $p = q$ the roots $\pm e_i$ do not occur and the system is $C_q$. A short count gives $\rho = (p+q-1, p+q-3, \dots, p-q+1)$. The diagonal torus $T = \mathrm{U}(1)^{p+q}$ lies in $K$ and is a Cartan subgroup of $G$, so $G$ has a compact Cartan subgroup.

## Admissible representations and $(\mathfrak{g}, K)$-modules

**Definition.** A *representation* of $G$ on a complete locally convex space $V$ (a Banach, Hilbert or Fréchet space) is a homomorphism $\pi : G \to \mathrm{GL}(V)$ such that $G \times V \to V$, $(g, v) \mapsto \pi(g) v$, is continuous. It is a *[[unitary representation|74BPYJNZTM]]* if $V$ is a Hilbert space and each $\pi(g)$ is unitary.

For $\tau$ in the set $\widehat{K}$ of classes of irreducible representations of $K$, let $V(\tau)$ be the *$\tau$-isotypic subspace*, the closed span of the images of $K$-maps $\tau \to V$. The operator $E_\tau = \dim \tau \int_K \overline{\chi_\tau(k)}\, \pi(k)\, dk$ is a continuous projection onto $V(\tau)$ ([isotypic components](/learn/representations/#compact-groups)).

**Definition.** A vector $v \in V$ is *$K$-finite* if $\pi(K) v$ spans a finite-dimensional space, and *smooth* if $g \mapsto \pi(g) v$ is smooth. Let $V_K$ be the space of $K$-finite vectors, which is the algebraic sum of the $V(\tau)$, and $V^\infty$ the space of smooth vectors. The representation is *admissible* if $\dim \mathrm{Hom}_K(\tau, V) < \infty$ for each $\tau \in \widehat{K}$.

The space $V^\infty$ is dense in $V$ and stable under $\mathfrak{g}$, which acts by $d\pi(X) v = \frac{d}{dt}\big|_{t=0} \pi(\exp tX) v$ ([Lie groups and Lie algebras](/learn/analysis/#lie-groups-and-lie-algebras)). This action extends to the [[universal enveloping algebra|SCZ8PRVY00]] $U(\mathfrak{g})$.

**Proposition (K-finite vectors).** Let $\pi$ be admissible. Then each $V(\tau)$ is finite-dimensional and consists of smooth vectors, $V_K$ is dense in $V$, and $d\pi(\mathfrak{g}) V_K \subseteq V_K$.

*Proof.* Admissibility makes $V(\tau)$ finite-dimensional. For $f \in C_c^\infty(G)$, the vector $\pi(f) v = \int_G f(g) \pi(g) v\, dg$ is smooth, and these vectors are dense (Gårding). The operator $E_\tau$ maps $\pi(f) v$ to $\pi(\chi * f) v$ for a smooth $\chi * f$, so $E_\tau$ maps a dense set of smooth vectors into $V(\tau)$. The image is dense in the finite-dimensional space $V(\tau)$, so it is all of $V(\tau)$, and $V(\tau) \subseteq V^\infty$. Density of $V_K$ follows from the Peter-Weyl theorem for $K$. For $v \in V(\tau)$ and $X \in \mathfrak{g}$, the map $X' \otimes v \mapsto d\pi(X') v$ from $\mathfrak{g} \otimes V(\tau)$ to $V$ is a $K$-map for the action $\mathrm{Ad} \otimes \pi$, so its image lies in the finite sum of the $V(\tau')$ with $\tau' \subseteq \mathfrak{g} \otimes \tau$. $\square$

This makes the following definition the algebraic shadow of an admissible representation.

**Definition.** A *$(\mathfrak{g}, K)$-module* is a complex vector space $V$ with a representation of the Lie algebra $\mathfrak{g}$ and a representation of the group $K$ such that:

1. each $v \in V$ lies in a finite-dimensional $K$-stable subspace on which $K$ acts continuously;
2. for $X \in \mathfrak{k}_0$ and $v \in V$, $Xv = \frac{d}{dt}\big|_{t=0} \exp(tX) v$;
3. for $k \in K$, $X \in \mathfrak{g}$ and $v \in V$, $k(Xv) = (\mathrm{Ad}(k) X)(kv)$.

A *morphism* is a linear map that commutes with $\mathfrak{g}$ and $K$. The module is *admissible* if each $\tau \in \widehat{K}$ occurs in $V$ with finite multiplicity, and *finitely generated* if it is finitely generated over $U(\mathfrak{g})$. A *[[Harish-Chandra module|2MKHH636C3]]* is an admissible finitely generated $(\mathfrak{g}, K)$-module. The $\tau \in \widehat{K}$ that occur in $V$ are the *$K$-types* of $V$.

**Theorem (Harish-Chandra).** Let $\pi$ be an admissible representation of $G$ on a Banach space $V$. Then $W \mapsto W_K$ is a bijection from the closed $G$-invariant subspaces of $V$ onto the $(\mathfrak{g}, K)$-submodules of $V_K$, with inverse $U \mapsto \overline{U}$. In particular $\pi$ is irreducible if and only if $V_K$ is an irreducible $(\mathfrak{g}, K)$-module.

See Knapp, *Representation Theory of Semisimple Groups*, Chapter VIII, and Wallach, *Real Reductive Groups I*, Chapter 3. The proof uses that $K$-finite vectors of an admissible representation are analytic (see the next section), so that the closure of a $(\mathfrak{g}, K)$-submodule is stable under $\exp(\mathfrak{g}_0)$ and $K$, which generate $G$.

**Theorem (Harish-Chandra admissibility theorem).** Each irreducible unitary representation of $G$ is admissible. If $G$ is connected, each $\tau \in \widehat{K}$ occurs in it at most $\dim \tau$ times.

See Knapp, *Representation Theory of Semisimple Groups*, VIII.1, for $G$ linear connected reductive. So the unitary dual is a set of irreducible Harish-Chandra modules with extra structure.

**Definition.** Admissible representations $\pi_1, \pi_2$ are *infinitesimally equivalent* if the $(\mathfrak{g}, K)$-modules $(V_1)_K$ and $(V_2)_K$ are isomorphic.

**Theorem (Harish-Chandra).** Two irreducible unitary representations of $G$ are unitarily equivalent if and only if they are infinitesimally equivalent.

See Knapp, *Representation Theory of Semisimple Groups*, Chapter VIII. The analogue for all admissible representations fails: the principal series of $\mathrm{SL}_2(\mathbb{R})$ on $L^p(K)$ for different $p$ are infinitesimally equivalent and not equivalent. This is why the development volumes state their local results for Harish-Chandra modules and pass to representations only through the [Casselman-Wallach theorem](/learn/representations/#representations-of-real-groups).

**Definition.** Let $X \mapsto \bar{X}$ be the complex conjugation of $\mathfrak{g}$ with respect to $\mathfrak{g}_0$. A Hermitian form $\langle\ ,\ \rangle$ on a $(\mathfrak{g}, K)$-module $V$ is *invariant* if $\langle k u, k v \rangle = \langle u, v \rangle$ and $\langle X u, v \rangle + \langle u, \bar{X} v \rangle = 0$ for $k \in K$, $X \in \mathfrak{g}$ and $u, v \in V$. The module is *unitarizable* if it has a positive definite invariant Hermitian form.

If $\pi$ is unitary, the inner product restricted to $V_K$ is invariant, because $d\pi(X)$ is skew-adjoint for $X \in \mathfrak{g}_0$.

**Theorem (unitarizable modules and the unitary dual; Harish-Chandra).** Let $V$ be an irreducible unitarizable Harish-Chandra module, and let $H$ be its completion for a positive definite invariant form. Then there is a unique irreducible unitary representation of $G$ on $H$ whose $K$-finite vectors are $V$, with the given actions of $\mathfrak{g}$ and $K$. So the [[unitary dual|95Q8WZMN0W]] $\widehat{G}$ is in bijection with the set of classes of irreducible unitarizable Harish-Chandra modules.

See Knapp, *Representation Theory of Semisimple Groups*, Chapter VIII, and Wallach, *Real Reductive Groups I*, Chapter 3. An irreducible $(\mathfrak{g}, K)$-module has countable dimension, so by [[Schur's lemma|X4M2FFKQJ6]] in the form of Dixmier, an invariant Hermitian form on it is unique up to a real scalar if it exists. The search for unitary representations is therefore a search for one form and a test of its sign on each $K$-type.

**Example ($K$-types of $\mathrm{SL}_2(\mathbb{R})$).** Let $G = \mathrm{SL}_2(\mathbb{R})$, $K = \mathrm{SO}(2) = \{k_\theta\}$ with $k_\theta = \begin{pmatrix} \cos\theta & \sin\theta \\ -\sin\theta & \cos\theta \end{pmatrix}$, so $\widehat{K} = \mathbb{Z}$ by $k_\theta \mapsto e^{in\theta}$. Put
$$H = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & -i \\ i & 0 \end{pmatrix}, \quad E = \frac{1}{2}\begin{pmatrix} 1 & i \\ i & -1 \end{pmatrix}, \quad F = \frac{1}{2}\begin{pmatrix} 1 & -i \\ -i & -1 \end{pmatrix}.$$
Then $[H, E] = 2E$, $[H, F] = -2F$, $[E, F] = H$, $\bar{E} = F$ and $\bar{H} = -H$. In a $(\mathfrak{g}, K)$-module, $H$ acts on the $K$-type $n$ by $n$, and $E$ and $F$ map the $K$-type $n$ to $n + 2$ and $n - 2$. So an irreducible $(\mathfrak{g}, K)$-module of $\mathrm{SL}_2(\mathbb{R})$ has $K$-types in one class modulo $2$, and we show below that each occurs at most once.

## The center of $U(\mathfrak{g})$ and the Casimir operator

Let $\mathcal{Z}(\mathfrak{g})$ be the center of $U(\mathfrak{g})$. The universal enveloping algebra and its center act on each $(\mathfrak{g}, K)$-module, and the center acts by $(\mathfrak{g}, K)$-endomorphisms, because $\mathrm{Ad}(K)$ fixes $\mathcal{Z}(\mathfrak{g})$ when $G$ is connected, and in our examples also when it is not.

**Definition (Casimir element).** Let $B$ be a nondegenerate symmetric bilinear form on $\mathfrak{g}_0$ that is invariant, $B([Y, X], X') + B(X, [Y, X']) = 0$, and also invariant under $\mathrm{Ad}(G)$. Let $X_1, \dots, X_d$ be a basis of $\mathfrak{g}_0$ and $X^1, \dots, X^d$ the dual basis, $B(X_i, X^j) = \delta_{ij}$. The *Casimir element* is $\Omega_B = \sum_i X_i X^i \in U(\mathfrak{g})$.

**Proposition.** $\Omega_B$ does not depend on the basis and lies in $\mathcal{Z}(\mathfrak{g})$.

*Proof.* The element $\sum_i X_i \otimes X^i \in \mathfrak{g}_0 \otimes \mathfrak{g}_0$ is the image of $B^{-1}$, so it does not depend on the basis. For $Y \in \mathfrak{g}_0$, write $[Y, X_i] = \sum_j c_{ji} X_j$; invariance of $B$ gives $[Y, X^i] = -\sum_j c_{ij} X^j$. Then $[Y, \Omega_B] = \sum_i [Y, X_i] X^i + X_i [Y, X^i] = \sum_{i,j} c_{ji} X_j X^i - \sum_{i,j} c_{ij} X_i X^j = 0$. $\square$

**Example.** For $\mathfrak{gl}_n$ and $B(X, Y) = \operatorname{tr}(XY)$, with the basis $E_{ij}$ and dual basis $E_{ji}$, $\Omega = \sum_{i,j} E_{ij} E_{ji}$. For $\mathfrak{sl}_2$ and $B(X, Y) = \frac{1}{2}\operatorname{tr}(XY)$, in the basis $H, E, F$ of the example on $K$-types of $\mathrm{SL}_2(\mathbb{R})$, the dual basis is $H, 2F, 2E$, so $\Omega_B = H^2 + 2EF + 2FE = H^2 + 2H + 4FE$. We write $\Omega$ for this element.

**Theorem (Gelfand).** The center $\mathcal{Z}(\mathfrak{gl}_n)$ is a polynomial algebra in the $n$ elements
$$C_k = \sum_{i_1, \dots, i_k = 1}^n E_{i_1 i_2} E_{i_2 i_3} \cdots E_{i_k i_1}, \qquad 1 \le k \le n.$$

This is Gelfand, *The center of an infinitesimal group ring*, Mat. Sbornik 26 (1950). The operators $\Delta_{\tau, k}$ of the finiteness theorem `HarishChandraFinite` in the development volumes are the right actions of these elements at each archimedean place. A second set of generators is given by the coefficients of the Capelli determinant (Howe and Umeda, Math. Ann. 290 (1991)); the development volumes call it the *quantum determinant* and read the infinitesimal character from it.

**Theorem (Harish-Chandra isomorphism).** Let $\mathfrak{h} \subseteq \mathfrak{g}$ be a Cartan subalgebra with Weyl group $W = W(\mathfrak{g}, \mathfrak{h})$. There is an algebra isomorphism $\gamma : \mathcal{Z}(\mathfrak{g}) \to S(\mathfrak{h})^W$ such that $z$ acts on each highest weight module of highest weight $\mu$ (for a positive system with half sum $\rho_\mathfrak{h}$) by the value of $\gamma(z)$ at $\mu + \rho_\mathfrak{h}$.

See Humphreys, *Introduction to Lie Algebras and Representation Theory*, §23, and Knapp, *Lie Groups Beyond an Introduction*, Chapter V. So the characters of $\mathcal{Z}(\mathfrak{g})$ are the maps $\chi_\lambda : z \mapsto \gamma(z)(\lambda)$, $\lambda \in \mathfrak{h}^*$, and $\chi_\lambda = \chi_{\lambda'}$ if and only if $\lambda' \in W\lambda$ ([[Harish-Chandra isomorphism|YNBQS0X9ZB]]).

**Definition.** A $(\mathfrak{g}, K)$-module $V$ has *infinitesimal character* $\lambda \in \mathfrak{h}^*/W$ if each $z \in \mathcal{Z}(\mathfrak{g})$ acts on $V$ by the scalar $\chi_\lambda(z)$. It is *$\mathcal{Z}(\mathfrak{g})$-finite* if $\mathcal{Z}(\mathfrak{g}) v$ is finite-dimensional for each $v$, which holds if an ideal of finite codimension kills $V$. The character $\lambda$ is *integral* if $\langle \lambda, \alpha^\vee \rangle \in \mathbb{Z}$ for each root $\alpha$, and *regular* if $\langle \lambda, \alpha^\vee \rangle \ne 0$ for each root.

For $\mathfrak{gl}_n$, $\mathfrak{h}$ is the diagonal, $W = S_n$, and an infinitesimal character is a multiset of $n$ complex numbers. The finite-dimensional representation of highest weight $\mu$ has infinitesimal character $\{\mu_k + \frac{n+1}{2} - k\}$.

**Proposition.** An irreducible Harish-Chandra module has an infinitesimal character. If the form $B$ restricts to $\mathfrak{h}$ with dual form $\langle\ ,\ \rangle$ on $\mathfrak{h}^*$, then $\Omega_B$ acts on a module with infinitesimal character $\lambda$ by $\langle \lambda, \lambda \rangle - \langle \rho_\mathfrak{h}, \rho_\mathfrak{h} \rangle$.

*Proof.* An irreducible $(\mathfrak{g}, K)$-module $V$ has countable dimension, and each $z \in \mathcal{Z}(\mathfrak{g})$ is an endomorphism of it, so $z$ acts by a scalar (Dixmier's form of Schur's lemma). For the value of $\Omega_B$ it suffices, by the theorem above, to compute it on a highest weight vector $v$ of weight $\mu$. Choose the basis of $\mathfrak{g}$ made of an orthonormal basis $H_i$ of $\mathfrak{h}$ and root vectors $X_\alpha$, $X_{-\alpha}$ with $B(X_\alpha, X_{-\alpha}) = 1$. Then $\Omega_B = \sum_i H_i^2 + \sum_{\alpha > 0} (X_\alpha X_{-\alpha} + X_{-\alpha} X_\alpha) = \sum_i H_i^2 + \sum_{\alpha > 0} (2 X_{-\alpha} X_\alpha + H_\alpha)$, where $H_\alpha = [X_\alpha, X_{-\alpha}]$ corresponds to $\alpha$ under $B$. On $v$ this gives $\langle \mu, \mu \rangle + \langle \mu, 2\rho_\mathfrak{h} \rangle = \langle \mu + \rho_\mathfrak{h}, \mu + \rho_\mathfrak{h} \rangle - \langle \rho_\mathfrak{h}, \rho_\mathfrak{h} \rangle$. $\square$

**Example ($\mathrm{SL}_2(\mathbb{R})$).** With $\Omega = H^2 + 2H + 4FE$, a module with infinitesimal character $s$ (that is, $\pm s$, with $\rho = 1$) has $\Omega = s^2 - 1$. On a vector $v$ of $K$-type $n$ this gives
$$4FEv = (s^2 - (n+1)^2) v.$$
So $Ev = 0$ is possible only if $s = \pm(n + 1)$, and likewise $Fv = 0$ only if $s = \pm(n - 1)$. On $K$-invariant functions on $G$, that is on functions on $\mathbb{H} = G/K$ under $g \mapsto g \cdot i$, the right action of $\Omega$ is $4 y^2 (\partial_x^2 + \partial_y^2)$. So a Maass form of Laplace eigenvalue $\lambda = \frac{1}{4}(1 - s^2)$ generates a representation with infinitesimal character $s$, and Selberg's conjecture $\lambda \ge \frac{1}{4}$ says $s \in i\mathbb{R}$.

**Theorem (Harish-Chandra finiteness for Z(g)-finite modules).** Let $V$ be a $(\mathfrak{g}, K)$-module that is finitely generated and $\mathcal{Z}(\mathfrak{g})$-finite. Then $V$ is admissible and has finite length.

See Wallach, *Real Reductive Groups I*, Chapter 3. Conversely an admissible finitely generated module is $\mathcal{Z}(\mathfrak{g})$-finite, since $\mathcal{Z}(\mathfrak{g})$ preserves each finite-dimensional $V(\tau)$. So the Harish-Chandra modules are exactly the finitely generated $\mathcal{Z}(\mathfrak{g})$-finite $(\mathfrak{g}, K)$-modules. The development volumes use this in the form: a space of $K$-finite and $\mathcal{Z}(\mathfrak{g})$-finite functions that is stable under $\mathfrak{g}$ and $K$ and generated by finitely many of them has finite multiplicities.

**Theorem (analyticity of K-finite matrix coefficients; Harish-Chandra).** Let $\pi$ be an admissible representation of finite length. Then each $v \in V_K$ is an analytic vector, and each $K$-finite [[matrix coefficients|J85Q7QGGHS]] $g \mapsto \lambda(\pi(g) v)$, with $v \in V_K$ and $\lambda$ a $K$-finite continuous linear form, is real analytic on $G$.

*Sketch of proof.* Take $B$ positive definite on $\mathfrak{p}_0$ and negative definite on $\mathfrak{k}_0$ (the trace form for $\mathrm{GL}_n$), with orthonormal bases $X_i$ of $\mathfrak{p}_0$ and $Y_j$ of $\mathfrak{k}_0$ in the sense $B(X_i, X_i) = 1$, $B(Y_j, Y_j) = -1$. Then $\Omega_B = \sum X_i^2 - \sum Y_j^2$, and $\Omega_B + 2 \sum Y_j^2 = \sum X_i^2 + \sum Y_j^2$ is an elliptic operator with analytic coefficients on $G$. A $K$-finite $\mathcal{Z}(\mathfrak{g})$-finite function $f$ spans a finite-dimensional space under $\Omega_B$ and under the Casimir $\sum Y_j^2$ of $K$, so $f$ is a solution of $P(D) f = 0$ for a polynomial $P$ in this elliptic operator. Elliptic regularity makes $f$ real analytic. $\square$

This is the fact `harishChandraAnalyticity` of the [third place hypotheses](/step/unitary-group-third-place-hypotheses-def/), and the same argument gives the lemma of [Harish-Chandra finiteness](/learn/trace-formula/#finiteness-of-automorphic-forms).

## Principal series and the subrepresentation theorem

**Definition (induced representations).** Let $P = MAN$ be a standard parabolic subgroup, $(\sigma, H_\sigma)$ an admissible representation of $M$ on a Hilbert space, and $\nu \in \mathfrak{a}^*_\mathbb{C}$. The *induced representation* $I(P, \sigma, \nu)$ acts by right translation on the completion of the space of continuous $f : G \to H_\sigma$ with
$$f(man g) = e^{(\nu + \rho_P)(\log a)} \sigma(m) f(g)$$
for the norm $\|f\|^2 = \int_K \|f(k)\|^2\, dk$. If $P = P_0$, these are the *principal series*.

This is the real form of [[parabolic induction|JJP4JFH3C6]]. The shift by $\rho_P$ is the normalization, and it gives the following properties. By the Iwasawa decomposition, restriction to $K$ identifies $I(P, \sigma, \nu)$ with a space of functions on $K$ that does not depend on $\nu$ (the *compact picture*).

**Proposition.** (1) As a representation of $K$, $I(P, \sigma, \nu)$ is induced from $\sigma|_{K \cap M}$; so by Frobenius reciprocity it is admissible when $\sigma$ is, and its $K$-types do not depend on $\nu$. (2) If $\sigma$ is unitary and $\nu \in i\mathfrak{a}^*$, then $I(P, \sigma, \nu)$ is unitary. (3) If $\sigma$ has infinitesimal character $\lambda_\sigma$ (for a Cartan subalgebra $\mathfrak{h}_M$ of $\mathfrak{m}$), then $I(P, \sigma, \nu)$ has infinitesimal character $(\lambda_\sigma, \nu)$ on $\mathfrak{h}_M \oplus \mathfrak{a}$.

See Knapp, *Representation Theory of Semisimple Groups*, Chapter VII. For (2): with $\nu$ imaginary, $\|f(g)\|^2$ transforms on the left under $P$ by $\delta_P$, and $\int_K$ is the $G$-invariant functional on such functions.

**Definition (intertwining operators).** Let $\bar{N} = \theta(N)$. For $\operatorname{Re} \nu$ in the positive chamber of $\mathfrak{a}^*$ far enough from the walls, the integral
$$A(\bar{P} : P : \sigma : \nu) f(g) = \int_{\bar{N}} f(\bar{n} g)\, d\bar{n}$$
converges and defines a $G$-map $I(P, \sigma, \nu) \to I(\bar{P}, \sigma, \nu)$. It continues meromorphically in $\nu$ (Knapp and Stein).

See Knapp, *Representation Theory of Semisimple Groups*, Chapter VII. These operators produce the invariant Hermitian forms on the complementary series below, and their images are the Langlands quotients of the last sections.

**Theorem (Casselman subrepresentation theorem).** Each irreducible admissible $(\mathfrak{g}, K)$-module is isomorphic to a submodule of $I(P_0, \sigma, \nu)_K$ for some irreducible representation $\sigma$ of $M_0$ and some $\nu \in \mathfrak{a}_0^*{}_\mathbb{C}$.

See Casselman and Miličić, *Asymptotic behavior of matrix coefficients of admissible representations*, Duke Math. J. 49 (1982), and Knapp, *Representation Theory of Semisimple Groups*, Chapter VIII. Both say that the principal series contain every irreducible module. With part (3) of the proposition, there are only finitely many irreducible Harish-Chandra modules with a given infinitesimal character.

**Example (principal series of $\mathrm{SL}_2(\mathbb{R})$).** Let $P$ be the upper triangular group, $M = \{\pm 1\}$, $A = \{\operatorname{diag}(a, a^{-1}) : a > 0\}$. For a character $\varepsilon \in \{+, -\}$ of $M$ and $s \in \mathbb{C}$, write $I^\varepsilon(s)$ for $I(P, \varepsilon, s\rho)$. Its $K$-types are the even integers if $\varepsilon = +$ and the odd integers if $\varepsilon = -$, each once. Its infinitesimal character is $s$, so $\Omega = s^2 - 1$. By the formula $4FEv = (s^2 - (n+1)^2)v$, $E$ and $F$ are injective between consecutive $K$-types unless $s \pm (n+1) = 0$ for some $n$ of the right parity. So $I^\varepsilon(s)$ is irreducible unless $s \in \mathbb{Z}$ and $\varepsilon = (-1)^{s+1}$. At these points:

- for $s = k - 1 \ge 1$ and $\varepsilon = (-1)^k$, the $K$-types $n \ge k$ and $n \le -k$ form two submodules $D_k^+$ and $D_k^-$, the *discrete series*, and the quotient is the finite-dimensional representation of dimension $k - 1$ (for $k \ge 2$);
- for $s = 0$ and $\varepsilon = -$, $I^-(0) = D_1^+ \oplus D_1^-$, the *limits of discrete series*;
- for $s = -(k - 1)$ the roles of sub and quotient are exchanged.

Each irreducible Harish-Chandra module of $\mathrm{SL}_2(\mathbb{R})$ is one of these (Knapp, *Representation Theory of Semisimple Groups*, Chapter II).

**Theorem (Bargmann classification).** The irreducible unitary representations of $\mathrm{SL}_2(\mathbb{R})$ are: the trivial representation; $I^+(s)$ for $s \in i\mathbb{R}$; $I^-(s)$ for $s \in i\mathbb{R}$, $s \ne 0$; the *complementary series* $I^+(s)$ for $0 < s < 1$; $D_k^\pm$ for $k \ge 2$; and $D_1^\pm$. The only equivalences are $I^\varepsilon(s) \cong I^\varepsilon(-s)$.

*Proof of the unitarity condition.* Let $V$ be one of the irreducible modules above, with $\Omega = s^2 - 1$, and suppose $\langle\ ,\ \rangle$ is a positive definite invariant form. Since $\bar{\Omega} = \Omega$, $\Omega$ is symmetric for the form, so $s^2 \in \mathbb{R}$. Since $\bar{E} = F$, for $v$ of $K$-type $n$,
$$\|Ev\|^2 = -\langle v, FEv \rangle = \tfrac{1}{4}((n+1)^2 - s^2) \|v\|^2.$$
If $n$ and $n + 2$ are both $K$-types of $V$, then $Ev \ne 0$, so $(n+1)^2 > s^2$. For $I^+(s)$ irreducible this is needed at $n = 0$, so $s^2 < 1$: $s \in i\mathbb{R}$ or $-1 < s < 1$. For $I^-(s)$ it is needed at $n = -1$, so $s^2 < 0$. For $D_k^+$ the $K$-types are $n \ge k$ and $s = k - 1$, so the condition holds. For the finite-dimensional representations of dimension at least $2$ it fails. Conversely, when the condition holds on all pairs of consecutive $K$-types, the displayed formula defines a positive form inductively from one $K$-type, and it is invariant. Bargmann, *Irreducible unitary representations of the Lorentz group*, Ann. of Math. 48 (1947), has the full argument. $\square$

The complementary series $0 < s < 1$ correspond, through the example of the last section, to Laplace eigenvalues $0 < \lambda < \frac{1}{4}$. Over $\mathrm{GL}_n$ their analogues are the only obstruction between unitarity and temperedness, as the last section shows.

## Asymptotics of matrix coefficients

In this section $G$ has compact center. For $\mathrm{GL}_n$ one applies the results to $G^1 = \{g : |\det g| = 1\}$, or to representations with unitary central character modulo the center.

**Definition (Harish-Chandra's $\Xi$ function).** For $g \in G$ let $H(g) \in \mathfrak{a}_0$ be defined by $g \in N_0 \exp(H(g)) K$. The function $f_0(g) = e^{\rho(H(g))}$ is the $K$-invariant vector of $I(P_0, 1, 0)$ with $f_0(1) = 1$, and
$$\Xi(g) = \langle I(P_0, 1, 0)(g) f_0, f_0 \rangle = \int_K e^{\rho(H(kg))}\, dk.$$
For $g = k \exp(H) k'$ with $H \in \overline{\mathfrak{a}_0^+}$, put $\sigma(g) = \|H\|$, for a fixed norm on $\mathfrak{a}_0$.

The Harish-Chandra Xi function $\Xi$ is a spherical function, bi-invariant under $K$, positive, with $\Xi(1) = 1$ and $\Xi(g^{-1}) = \Xi(g)$. It is a matrix coefficient of a tempered unitary representation, and it measures the slowest decay that a tempered coefficient can have.

**Theorem (Harish-Chandra).** There are $c, C > 0$ and $d \ge 0$ such that for all $H \in \overline{\mathfrak{a}_0^+}$,
$$c\, e^{-\rho(H)} \le \Xi(\exp H) \le C (1 + \|H\|)^d e^{-\rho(H)}.$$

See Harish-Chandra, *Harmonic analysis on real reductive groups I*, J. Funct. Anal. 19 (1975), and Wallach, *Real Reductive Groups I*, Chapter 4.

**Corollary.** (1) For each $\varepsilon > 0$, $\Xi \in L^{2 + \varepsilon}(G)$. (2) For $r$ large enough, $\int_G \Xi(g)^2 (1 + \sigma(g))^{-r}\, dg < \infty$. (3) $\Xi \notin L^2(G)$ unless $G$ is compact.

*Proof.* By the KAK integration formula of the first section, $\int_G \phi\, dg \le \int_{\mathfrak{a}_0^+} \phi(\exp H) e^{2\rho(H)}\, dH$ for $K$-bi-invariant $\phi \ge 0$. The element $\rho$ is a combination of the simple roots with positive coefficients, so $\rho(H) \ge c' \|H\|$ on $\overline{\mathfrak{a}_0^+}$ for some $c' > 0$. For (1), $\Xi^{2+\varepsilon} e^{2\rho} \le C' (1 + \|H\|)^{d'} e^{-\varepsilon \rho(H)}$, which is integrable. For (2), $\Xi^2 (1 + \|H\|)^{-r} e^{2\rho} \le C^2 (1 + \|H\|)^{2d - r}$, integrable for $r > 2d + \dim \mathfrak{a}_0$. For (3), the lower bounds give $\Xi^2 \cdot \prod \sinh(\alpha(H))^{m_\alpha} \ge c''$ on a translate of the chamber. $\square$

Part (2) is the form of the estimate that the development volumes use to show that integrals of tempered coefficients over subgroups converge. For example, the local Gan-Gross-Prasad period $\int_H \langle \pi(h) u, v \rangle\, dh$ converges absolutely for tempered $\pi$ (Ichino and Ikeda, *On the periods of automorphic forms on special orthogonal groups and the Gross-Prasad conjecture*, Geom. Funct. Anal. 19 (2010), for orthogonal groups; N. Harris, *The refined Gross-Prasad conjecture for unitary groups*, Int. Math. Res. Not. 2014, for unitary groups). The function $\Xi$ of the development statement `XiExists` is a majorant of this kind, adapted to a Bessel subgroup.

**Theorem (asymptotic expansion; Harish-Chandra, Casselman and Miličić).** Let $\pi$ be an admissible representation of finite length. There are a finite set $E \subseteq \mathfrak{a}_0^*{}_\mathbb{C}$ and an integer $D$ with this property. For $v \in V_K$ and a $K$-finite continuous linear form $\lambda$, there are polynomials $p_{\nu, m}$ on $\mathfrak{a}_0$ of degree at most $D$, for $\nu \in E$ and $m \in \mathbb{Z}_{\ge 0}^\Delta$, such that for $H \in \mathfrak{a}_0^+$
$$\lambda(\pi(\exp H) v) = \sum_{\nu \in E} \sum_{m} p_{\nu, m}(H)\, e^{(\nu - \rho - \sum_{\alpha \in \Delta} m_\alpha \alpha)(H)},$$
with absolute convergence, uniform on each set $\{H : \alpha(H) \ge \varepsilon \text{ for } \alpha \in \Delta\}$, $\varepsilon > 0$.

See Casselman and Miličić, Duke Math. J. 49 (1982), and Wallach, *Real Reductive Groups I*, Chapter 4. The proof writes the coefficient as a solution of a system of differential equations on $A_0^+$, regular singular at infinity, coming from the action of $\mathcal{Z}(\mathfrak{g})$. We take $E$ minimal; its elements are the *exponents* of $\pi$. The maximal ones, for the order $\nu \ge \nu'$ if $\nu - \nu'$ is a sum of simple roots, are the *leading exponents*. Casselman showed that they are read from the action of $\mathfrak{a}_0$ on the Jacquet module of $V_K$, the space of $\mathfrak{n}_0$-coinvariants, which is finite-dimensional.

**Theorem (Casselman criterion; Langlands, Casselman and Miličić).** Let $\pi$ be an irreducible admissible representation of $G$, with $G$ of compact center. Then:

1. $\pi$ is a [[discrete series|9AB7HYGPEK]] (its $K$-finite matrix coefficients are in $L^2(G)$) if and only if $\operatorname{Re} \nu(H) < 0$ for each exponent $\nu$ and each nonzero $H \in \overline{\mathfrak{a}_0^+}$;
2. $\pi$ is tempered (definition below) if and only if $\operatorname{Re} \nu(H) \le 0$ for each exponent $\nu$ and each $H \in \overline{\mathfrak{a}_0^+}$.

See Casselman and Miličić, Duke Math. J. 49 (1982), and Knapp, *Representation Theory of Semisimple Groups*, Chapter VIII. Since $\operatorname{Re} \nu(H) < 0$ on the closed chamber means that $-\operatorname{Re} \nu$ is a combination of simple roots with positive coefficients, (1) says: the leading exponents lie strictly inside the negative obtuse cone. The direction of the proof from exponents to $L^2$ is the integral formula: $|\lambda(\pi(\exp H)v)|^2 e^{2\rho(H)}$ is a sum of terms $|p(H)|^2 e^{2 \operatorname{Re} \nu(H)}$.

**Definition (tempered).** An admissible representation $\pi$ of finite length is *tempered* if for each $v \in V_K$ and each $K$-finite $\lambda$ there are $C$ and $d$ with
$$|\lambda(\pi(g) v)| \le C\, \Xi(g) (1 + \sigma(g))^d \quad \text{for all } g \in G.$$

**Theorem.** For an irreducible unitary representation $\pi$ of $G$, with $G$ of compact center, these are equivalent: (a) $\pi$ is tempered; (b) its $K$-finite matrix coefficients lie in $L^{2+\varepsilon}(G)$ for each $\varepsilon > 0$; (c) $\pi$ is weakly contained in the regular representation $L^2(G)$.

The step from (a) to (b) is part (1) of the corollary above, and the step back uses the exponents. The equivalence of (b) and (c) is Cowling, Haagerup and Howe, *Almost $L^2$ matrix coefficients*, J. Reine Angew. Math. 387 (1988). By Harish-Chandra's Plancherel theorem, the support of the [[Plancherel measure|6JGAG0KN98]] is the set of irreducible tempered unitary representations.

**Example ($\mathrm{SL}_2(\mathbb{R})$).** Take $a_t = \operatorname{diag}(e^t, e^{-t})$, so $\alpha(\log a_t) = 2t$ and $\rho(\log a_t) = t$. A unit vector $v$ of $K$-type $k$ in $D_k^+$ has $|\langle D_k^+(a_t) v, v \rangle| = (\cosh t)^{-k}$. The exponent is $\nu = -(k-1)\rho$: square integrable for $k \ge 2$, tempered for $k = 1$. The spherical function of $I^+(s)$, $0 < \operatorname{Re} s$, behaves as $c(s) e^{(s - 1)t}$, so its leading exponent is $s\rho$. Hence $I^+(s)$ is tempered exactly when $s \in i\mathbb{R}$, and the complementary series are unitary and not tempered: their coefficients lie in $L^p$ only for $p > 2/(1 - s)$.

## Discrete series and formal degrees

In this section $G$ is connected with compact center, as $\mathrm{U}(p, q)$ and $\mathrm{SU}(1, 1) \cong \mathrm{SL}_2(\mathbb{R})$ are. For $\mathrm{GL}_2(\mathbb{R})$ we work modulo the center.

**Definition.** An irreducible unitary representation $\pi$ of $G$ is *square integrable*, or a *discrete series* representation, if it has a nonzero matrix coefficient in $L^2(G)$.

**Theorem (Godement, Harish-Chandra).** Let $\pi$ be irreducible unitary. These are equivalent: (a) $\pi$ is square integrable; (b) all matrix coefficients of $\pi$ are in $L^2(G)$; (c) $\pi$ is equivalent to a closed $G$-invariant subspace of $L^2(G)$ under right translation. Then there is $d(\pi) > 0$, the *formal degree*, with
$$\int_G \langle \pi(g) u, v \rangle \overline{\langle \pi(g) u', v' \rangle}\, dg = d(\pi)^{-1} \langle u, u' \rangle \overline{\langle v, v' \rangle}.$$

This is the [Schur orthogonality for discrete series](/learn/representations/#discrete-series); see Knapp, *Representation Theory of Semisimple Groups*, Chapter IX. The formal degree depends on the Haar measure, and the product $d(\pi)\, dg$ does not. It is the field `schurOrthogonalitySquareIntegrable` of [ThetaLocalInputs](/step/theta-local-inputs-def/), and the theta branch uses it to compute local inner products of theta lifts.

**Definition (distribution character).** Let $\pi$ be admissible of finite length. For $f \in C_c^\infty(G)$, the operator $\pi(f)$ is of trace class, and $\Theta_\pi(f) = \operatorname{tr} \pi(f)$ is a distribution on $G$, the *character* of $\pi$.

By Harish-Chandra's regularity theorem, $\Theta_\pi$ is a locally integrable function, real analytic on the set $G'$ of regular semisimple elements. Irreducible Harish-Chandra modules with the same character are isomorphic (Knapp, *Representation Theory of Semisimple Groups*, Chapter X).

Assume now that $G$ has a compact Cartan subgroup $T \subseteq K$, with Lie algebra $\mathfrak{t}_0$ and $\mathfrak{t} = \mathfrak{t}_0 \otimes \mathbb{C}$, a Cartan subalgebra of $\mathfrak{g}$. The roots $\Delta(\mathfrak{g}, \mathfrak{t})$ are imaginary on $\mathfrak{t}_0$. A root is *compact* if its root space lies in $\mathfrak{k}$ and *noncompact* if it lies in $\mathfrak{p}$. Let $W = W(\mathfrak{g}, \mathfrak{t})$ and $W_K = W(\mathfrak{k}, \mathfrak{t})$, a subgroup of $W$. Let $q = \frac{1}{2} \dim G/K$.

**Theorem (Harish-Chandra classification of discrete series).** $G$ has discrete series if and only if it has a compact Cartan subgroup. In that case, for each $\lambda \in i\mathfrak{t}_0^*$ that is regular and such that $\lambda + \rho$ is the differential of a character of $T$ (for the positive system $\Delta^+$ that makes $\lambda$ dominant), there is a discrete series $\pi_\lambda$ with infinitesimal character $\lambda$, whose character on $T \cap G'$ is
$$\Theta_{\pi_\lambda} = (-1)^q \frac{\sum_{w \in W_K} \operatorname{sgn}(w) e^{w\lambda}}{\prod_{\alpha \in \Delta^+} (e^{\alpha/2} - e^{-\alpha/2})}.$$
Each discrete series is some $\pi_\lambda$, and $\pi_\lambda \cong \pi_{\lambda'}$ if and only if $\lambda' \in W_K \lambda$.

See Harish-Chandra, *Discrete series for semisimple Lie groups II*, Acta Math. 116 (1966), and Knapp, *Representation Theory of Semisimple Groups*, Chapters IX and XII. The element $\lambda$ is the *Harish-Chandra parameter* of $\pi_\lambda$. The $W$-orbit of a regular integral $\lambda$ splits into $|W / W_K|$ orbits of $W_K$. So each regular integral infinitesimal character is shared by exactly $|W|/|W_K|$ discrete series; they form one *L-packet*.

**Example ($\mathrm{SL}_2(\mathbb{R})$).** Here $T = K = \mathrm{SO}(2)$, the root $\alpha$ is $2$ (in the coordinate $n$ of $\widehat{K}$), $\rho = 1$, $W_K = 1$, $W = \{\pm 1\}$ and $q = 1$. The parameters are $\lambda \in \mathbb{Z} \setminus \{0\}$, and $\pi_{k-1} = D_k^+$, $\pi_{-(k-1)} = D_k^-$. On $K$, the formula gives $-e^{i(k-1)\theta} / (e^{i\theta} - e^{-i\theta}) = e^{ik\theta} / (1 - e^{2i\theta}) = \sum_{n \ge 0} e^{i(k+2n)\theta}$, the sum of the $K$-types of $D_k^+$.

**Theorem (Blattner formula and lowest K-type; Hecht and Schmid).** Let $\Delta^+$ make $\lambda$ dominant, let $\rho_c$ and $\rho_n$ be the half sums of the compact and of the noncompact positive roots, and let $Q(\mu)$ be the number of ways to write $\mu$ as a sum of noncompact positive roots. Then the multiplicity of the $K$-type of highest weight $\mu$ in $\pi_\lambda$ is
$$\sum_{w \in W_K} \operatorname{sgn}(w)\, Q\bigl(w(\mu + \rho_c) - \lambda - \rho_n\bigr).$$

See Hecht and Schmid, *A proof of Blattner's conjecture*, Invent. Math. 31 (1975). For $\mu = \Lambda = \lambda + \rho_n - \rho_c$ and $w = 1$ the term is $Q(0) = 1$, and the other terms vanish; this is the [lowest $K$-type theorem](/learn/representations/#discrete-series) of Schmid.

**Definition (minimal $K$-type; Vogan).** Fix a positive system for $\mathfrak{k}$ with half sum $\rho_c$. A $K$-type of highest weight $\mu$ of a Harish-Chandra module $V$ is *minimal* if $\|\mu + 2\rho_c\|$ is minimal among the $K$-types of $V$.

**Corollary.** The lowest $K$-type $\Lambda$ of $\pi_\lambda$ is its unique minimal $K$-type.

*Proof.* By the lowest $K$-type theorem each $K$-type of $\pi_\lambda$ has highest weight $\Lambda + \beta$, where $\beta$ is a sum of noncompact positive roots. Then $\|\Lambda + \beta + 2\rho_c\|^2 - \|\Lambda + 2\rho_c\|^2 = 2\langle \Lambda + 2\rho_c, \beta \rangle + \|\beta\|^2$. Here $\Lambda + 2\rho_c = \lambda + \rho_n + \rho_c = \lambda + \rho$ is regular dominant for $\Delta^+$, so $\langle \lambda + \rho, \beta \rangle > 0$ for $\beta \ne 0$. $\square$

Vogan's theory of minimal $K$-types (Vogan, *The algebraic structure of the representation of semisimple Lie groups I*, Ann. of Math. 109 (1979)) describes each irreducible Harish-Chandra module from its minimal $K$-types. The predicate `IsDS` of the development volumes defines a discrete series of $\mathrm{U}(p, q)$ by this property: the $K$-type $\Lambda$ occurs, and each $K$-type $\Lambda'$ has $\|\Lambda + 2\rho_c\| \le \|\Lambda' + 2\rho_c\|$.

**Theorem (formal degree; Harish-Chandra).** There is a constant $c_G > 0$, which depends only on $G$ and the Haar measure, such that
$$d(\pi_\lambda) = c_G \prod_{\alpha \in \Delta^+} |\langle \lambda, \alpha \rangle|.$$

See Harish-Chandra, Acta Math. 116 (1966). With the Haar measure that gives the compact dual group mass $1$, $d(\pi_\lambda)$ is the Weyl dimension $\prod_\alpha \langle \lambda, \alpha \rangle / \langle \rho, \alpha \rangle$ of the representation of the compact dual with infinitesimal character $\lambda$; this is the field `formalDegreeCompactDual` of [ThetaLocalInputs](/step/theta-local-inputs-def/).

**Example (formal degree of $D_k$).** Let $G = \mathrm{SL}_2(\mathbb{R})$, with Haar measure $\int_G f\, dg = \int_\mathbb{H} \int_K f(g_z k)\, dk\, d\mu(z)$, where $d\mu = y^{-2} dx\, dy$, $g_z \cdot i = z$ and $K$ has mass $1$. Then $d(D_k^\pm) = \frac{k - 1}{4\pi}$ for $k \ge 2$.

*Proof.* Realize $D_k^+$ on holomorphic $f$ on $\mathbb{H}$ with $\int |f|^2 y^k\, d\mu < \infty$. The lowest weight vector is a multiple of $(z + i)^{-k}$, the reproducing kernel at $i$, and the transformation law of the kernel gives $|\langle D_k^+(g) v, v \rangle| = \cosh(r/2)^{-k}$ for a unit vector $v$, where $r$ is the hyperbolic distance from $i$ to $g \cdot i$. In geodesic polar coordinates $d\mu = \sinh r\, dr\, d\phi$, so with $u = \cosh(r/2)$, $\sinh r\, dr = 4u\, du$ and
$$\int_G |\langle D_k^+(g) v, v \rangle|^2\, dg = 2\pi \int_1^\infty u^{-2k} \cdot 4u\, du = \frac{4\pi}{k - 1}.$$
By Schur orthogonality with $u = u' = v = v'$, this is $d(D_k^+)^{-1}$. $\square$

The integral diverges for $k = 1$: the limits of discrete series are tempered and not square integrable. The formula agrees with the general one, since $\langle \lambda, \alpha \rangle$ is proportional to $k - 1$.

**Example (discrete series of $\mathrm{U}(p, q)$).** Let $n = p + q$ and $G = \mathrm{U}(p, q)$, with $T$ the diagonal torus, $\mathfrak{t} = \mathbb{C}^n$, roots $e_a - e_b$, and $W = S_n$. The root $e_a - e_b$ is compact when $a, b \le p$ or $a, b > p$, so $W_K = S_p \times S_q$. A Harish-Chandra parameter is $\lambda \in \mathbb{R}^n$ with distinct entries in $\frac{n-1}{2} + \mathbb{Z}$, up to $S_p \times S_q$. The development volumes write it in a second form: order the entries $\mu_1 > \dots > \mu_n$, and record for each $a$ a sign $\varepsilon_a$ that says whether $\mu_a$ comes from the first $p$ or the last $q$ coordinates. Then the positive roots are $e_a - e_b$, $a < b$, the compact ones those with $\varepsilon_a = \varepsilon_b$, and
$$\rho_c(a) = \tfrac{1}{2}\bigl(\#\{b > a : \varepsilon_b = \varepsilon_a\} - \#\{b < a : \varepsilon_b = \varepsilon_a\}\bigr),$$
with $\rho_n(a)$ the same count over $b$ with $\varepsilon_b \ne \varepsilon_a$. The lowest $K$-type is $\Lambda = \mu + \rho_n - \rho_c$, and $d(\pi_\mu) = c_G \prod_{a < b} (\mu_a - \mu_b)$. For a regular integral infinitesimal character there are $\binom{n}{p}$ sign patterns with $p$ signs $+$, one for each discrete series in the L-packet. The *holomorphic* discrete series are the two patterns in which all $+$ entries come before all $-$ entries, or all after.

This is the form of the theorem `dsExhaust` of the development volumes, which shows that each square integrable irreducible representation of $\mathrm{U}(p, q)$ is a $\pi_\mu$ (the field `discreteSeriesClassification`). The [Bessel seesaw](/proof/bessel-seesaw/) restricts $\pi_\lambda$ of $\mathrm{U}(n+1)$-type groups to $\mathrm{U}(n)$-type groups, and its condition `Interlaced` is a condition on the merged order of two such sign patterns.

**Definition (limits of discrete series).** If $\lambda$ is singular but $\langle \lambda, \alpha \rangle \ne 0$ for each compact simple root of a positive system making $\lambda$ dominant, the same character formula defines a tempered irreducible (or zero, by an extra condition) representation, a *limit of discrete series*. The representations $D_1^\pm$ of $\mathrm{SL}_2(\mathbb{R})$ are the basic example.

See Knapp and Zuckerman, *Classification of irreducible tempered representations of semisimple groups*, Ann. of Math. 116 (1982).

**Theorem (cohomology of discrete series).** Let $F$ be an irreducible finite-dimensional representation of $G$, and $\pi$ an irreducible Harish-Chandra module. If $H^*(\mathfrak{g}, K; \pi \otimes F) \ne 0$, then $\pi$ has the infinitesimal character of $F^\vee$ (Wigner's lemma). If $\pi$ is a discrete series with this infinitesimal character, then $H^i(\mathfrak{g}, K; \pi \otimes F)$ is $\mathbb{C}$ for $i = q$ and $0$ otherwise.

See Borel and Wallach, *Continuous Cohomology, Discrete Subgroups, and Representations of Reductive Groups*, Chapters I and II. This is why discrete series with regular integral infinitesimal character appear at the real places of the [cohomological automorphic representations](/learn/automorphic-forms/#regular-algebraic-cohomological-and-polarized-representations) of unitary groups, which carry the Galois representations of the row.

## Tempered representations and the Langlands classification

We return to general $G$ as in the first section. For a standard parabolic subgroup $P = MAN$, the group $M$ is again of the same kind (with compact center modulo its connected component, and possibly disconnected), so the notions of the last two sections apply to $M$.

**Theorem (Harish-Chandra, Knapp and Zuckerman).** Let $P = MAN$ be a standard parabolic subgroup, $\sigma$ a discrete series of $M$, and $\nu \in \mathfrak{a}^*$ real. Then $I(P, \sigma, i\nu)$ is unitary, tempered, and a finite direct sum of irreducible representations. Each irreducible tempered representation of $G$ is a summand of such an $I(P, \sigma, i\nu)$.

See Knapp, *Representation Theory of Semisimple Groups*, Chapters VIII and XIV. Knapp and Zuckerman, Ann. of Math. 116 (1982), decide when $I(P, \sigma, i\nu)$ is reducible (by the *R-group*) and classify the tempered representations by data built from discrete series and limits of discrete series.

**Theorem ([[Langlands classification|YHFP9HGBTT]]).** Let $P = MAN$ be a standard parabolic subgroup, $\sigma$ an irreducible tempered representation of $M$, and $\nu \in \mathfrak{a}^*_\mathbb{C}$ with $\operatorname{Re} \nu$ in the open positive chamber: $\langle \operatorname{Re} \nu, \alpha \rangle > 0$ for each root $\alpha$ of $\mathfrak{a}$ in $\mathfrak{n}$. Then:

1. $I(P, \sigma, \nu)$ has a unique irreducible quotient $J(P, \sigma, \nu)$, the *Langlands quotient*, and it is the image of the intertwining operator $A(\bar{P} : P : \sigma : \nu)$;
2. each irreducible admissible representation of $G$ is infinitesimally equivalent to some $J(P, \sigma, \nu)$;
3. $J(P, \sigma, \nu) \cong J(P', \sigma', \nu')$ if and only if $P = P'$, $\sigma \cong \sigma'$ and $\nu = \nu'$.

See Langlands, *On the classification of irreducible representations of real algebraic groups*, in Representation Theory and Harmonic Analysis on Semisimple Lie Groups, Math. Surveys Monogr. 31 (1989); Borel and Wallach, Chapter IV; Knapp, *Representation Theory of Semisimple Groups*, Chapter VIII. The tempered representations are the case $P = G$. The proof uses the asymptotics of the last sections: the exponents of $J(P, \sigma, \nu)$ determine $\operatorname{Re} \nu$, as the leading term of the coefficients in the direction of $\mathfrak{a}$.

**Example ($\mathrm{SL}_2(\mathbb{R})$).** The trivial representation is $J(P, +, \rho)$, the finite-dimensional representation of dimension $k - 1$ is $J(P, (-1)^k, (k - 1)\rho)$, and the complementary series $I^+(s)$, $0 < s < 1$, is $J(P, +, s\rho)$ and equals its standard module.

For $\mathrm{GL}_n$ the classification has an explicit form. Let $F = \mathbb{R}$ or $\mathbb{C}$, $|z|_\mathbb{C} = z\bar{z}$, and $\nu = |\det|_F$. For representations $\pi_i$ of $\mathrm{GL}_{n_i}(F)$, let $\pi_1 \times \dots \times \pi_r$ be the normalized induction of $\pi_1 \otimes \dots \otimes \pi_r$ from the block upper triangular parabolic subgroup. A representation is *essentially square integrable* if it is $\delta \nu^s$ with $\delta$ square integrable modulo the center with unitary central character, and $s \in \mathbb{R}$.

**Example (discrete series of $\mathrm{GL}_n(F)$).** The group $\mathrm{GL}_n(F)$ modulo its center has a compact Cartan subgroup only for $F = \mathbb{R}$ and $n \le 2$, or $n = 1$. So the square integrable representations modulo the center are the unitary characters of $\mathbb{R}^\times$ and of $\mathbb{C}^\times$, and the representations $D_k \otimes |\det|^{it}$ of $\mathrm{GL}_2(\mathbb{R})$, $k \ge 2$, $t \in \mathbb{R}$. Here $D_k$ is the representation of $\mathrm{GL}_2(\mathbb{R})$ trivial on positive scalars whose restriction to $\mathrm{SL}_2(\mathbb{R})$ is $D_k^+ \oplus D_k^-$.

**Theorem (Langlands classification for $\mathrm{GL}_n(F)$, $F$ archimedean).** (1) If $\delta_1, \dots, \delta_r$ are square integrable modulo the center, then $\delta_1 \times \dots \times \delta_r$ is irreducible and tempered, and each irreducible tempered representation is of this form. (2) Let $\delta_i \nu^{s_i}$ be essentially square integrable with $s_1 \ge \dots \ge s_r$. Then $\delta_1 \nu^{s_1} \times \dots \times \delta_r \nu^{s_r}$ has a unique irreducible quotient. This gives a bijection between multisets $\{\delta_i \nu^{s_i}\}$ and irreducible admissible representations of $\mathrm{GL}_n(F)$.

See Knapp, *Local Langlands correspondence: the Archimedean case*, in Motives, Proc. Sympos. Pure Math. 55, Part 2 (1994). In this form the classification is parallel to the [p-adic Langlands classification](/learn/representations/#cuspidal-representations-and-segments), with the $\delta_i$ in the role of the segments.

**Remark (the archimedean local Langlands correspondence).** The Weil group of $\mathbb{R}$ is $W_\mathbb{R} = \mathbb{C}^\times \cup j\mathbb{C}^\times$ with $j^2 = -1$ and $jzj^{-1} = \bar{z}$; the Weil group of $\mathbb{C}$ is $\mathbb{C}^\times$. Langlands proved that the irreducible admissible representations of $\mathrm{GL}_n(F)$ correspond bijectively to the semisimple $n$-dimensional representations of $W_F$, compatibly with the classification: $\delta_1 \nu^{s_1} \times \dots$ goes to the direct sum of the parameters of the $\delta_i \nu^{s_i}$. The character $\chi$ of $F^\times$ goes to $\chi$ composed with $W_F^{ab} \cong F^\times$, and $D_k$ goes to $\mathrm{Ind}_{\mathbb{C}^\times}^{W_\mathbb{R}} (z / |z|)^{k-1}$. The infinitesimal character of $D_k$ is $\{\frac{k-1}{2}, -\frac{k-1}{2}\}$, the exponents of $(z/|z|)^{k-1} = z^{(k-1)/2} \bar{z}^{-(k-1)/2}$. This is the archimedean case of the [[local Langlands correspondence|9E45NBX1G0]] (Knapp, 1994, cited above), and it is how the Hodge-Tate weights of the [Galois row](/proof/galois-row/) match infinitesimal characters at the archimedean places.

## The unitary dual of $\mathrm{GL}_n$

Let $F$ be $\mathbb{R}$, $\mathbb{C}$ or a nonarchimedean local field, and keep the notation $\nu = |\det|_F$ and $\pi_1 \times \dots \times \pi_r$. For $F$ nonarchimedean, let $\mathcal{D}^u(F)$ be the set of classes of irreducible square integrable representations modulo the center, with unitary central character, of the groups $\mathrm{GL}_d(F)$, $d \ge 1$ ([segments](/learn/representations/#cuspidal-representations-and-segments)). For $F$ archimedean, let $\mathcal{D}^u(F)$ be the set of representations of the example above: unitary characters of $F^\times$, and for $F = \mathbb{R}$ also $D_k \otimes |\det|^{it}$.

**Definition (Speh representations).** For $\delta \in \mathcal{D}^u(F)$ and $m \ge 1$, let $u(\delta, m)$ be the unique irreducible quotient (the Langlands quotient) of
$$\delta \nu^{(m-1)/2} \times \delta \nu^{(m-3)/2} \times \dots \times \delta \nu^{-(m-1)/2}.$$

**Example.** For $\delta = \chi$ a unitary character of $F^\times$, $u(\chi, m) = \chi \circ \det$ is a character of $\mathrm{GL}_m(F)$. For $\delta = D_k$ of $\mathrm{GL}_2(\mathbb{R})$, $u(D_k, m)$ is a representation of $\mathrm{GL}_{2m}(\mathbb{R})$ studied by Speh, *Unitary representations of $\mathrm{GL}(n, \mathbb{R})$ with non-trivial $(\mathfrak{g}, K)$-cohomology*, Invent. Math. 71 (1983). For $m \ge 2$, $u(\delta, m)$ is unitary but not tempered, and it is not generic.

**Proposition (complementary series).** Let $\sigma$ be an irreducible unitary representation of $\mathrm{GL}_m(F)$ such that $\sigma \nu^\alpha \times \sigma \nu^{-\alpha}$ is irreducible for $0 \le \alpha < \frac{1}{2}$. Then $\sigma \nu^\alpha \times \sigma \nu^{-\alpha}$ is unitarizable for $0 < \alpha < \frac{1}{2}$.

*Sketch of proof.* For $\alpha = 0$ the representation is unitarily induced, hence unitary. For real $\alpha$ it is Hermitian, and its invariant Hermitian form depends continuously on $\alpha$ and is nondegenerate on each $K$-type, since the representation stays irreducible. So the signature on each $K$-type is constant on $[0, \frac{1}{2})$, and it is positive at $\alpha = 0$. $\square$

For $\sigma$ the trivial character of $\mathbb{R}^\times$, $\nu^\alpha \times \nu^{-\alpha}$ restricted to $\mathrm{SL}_2(\mathbb{R})$ is $I^+(2\alpha)$, and the interval $0 < \alpha < \frac{1}{2}$ is Bargmann's complementary series $0 < s < 1$.

**Theorem (unitary dual of GL(n); Tadić, Vogan).** Let
$$\mathcal{B}(F) = \{u(\delta, m),\ u(\delta, m) \nu^\alpha \times u(\delta, m) \nu^{-\alpha} : \delta \in \mathcal{D}^u(F),\ m \ge 1,\ 0 < \alpha < \tfrac{1}{2}\}.$$
Then:

1. each product $\tau_1 \times \dots \times \tau_r$ of elements of $\mathcal{B}(F)$ is irreducible and unitary;
2. each irreducible unitary representation of $\mathrm{GL}_n(F)$ is such a product;
3. the factors $\tau_i$ are unique up to order.

For $F$ nonarchimedean of characteristic $0$ this is the Tadić classification (Tadić, *Classification of unitary representations in irreducible representations of general linear group (non-Archimedean case)*, Ann. Sci. ENS 19 (1986)). It rests on Bernstein's theorem below. For $F$ archimedean the classification is Vogan, *The unitary dual of $\mathrm{GL}(n)$ over an archimedean field*, Invent. Math. 83 (1986). Tadić, *$\mathrm{GL}(n, \mathbb{C})^\wedge$ and $\mathrm{GL}(n, \mathbb{R})^\wedge$*, Contemp. Math. 489 (2009), shows that Vogan's answer has the same form as above. The unitarity of the Speh representations in the archimedean case is due to Speh (cited above) and to Sahi and Stein, *Analysis in matrix space and Speh's representations*, Invent. Math. 101 (1990).

**Theorem (Bernstein irreducibility theorem).** Let $F$ be nonarchimedean, and let $\sigma_1, \sigma_2$ be irreducible unitary representations of $\mathrm{GL}_{n_1}(F)$ and $\mathrm{GL}_{n_2}(F)$. Then $\sigma_1 \times \sigma_2$ is irreducible.

This is Bernstein, *P-invariant distributions on GL(N) and the classification of unitary representations of GL(N) (non-archimedean case)*, Lecture Notes in Math. 1041 (1984). The proof restricts to the mirabolic subgroup $P_n$ of matrices with last row $(0, \dots, 0, 1)$. Bernstein shows that a $P_n$-invariant distribution on $\mathrm{GL}_n(F)$, for the action by conjugation, is $\mathrm{GL}_n(F)$-invariant, and deduces that an irreducible unitary representation stays irreducible on $P_n$ (Kirillov's conjecture). The development chapter on the unitary dual follows this route: its theorem `ThmA` is the statement on distributions, and `ThmB` says that a $P_n$-invariant sesquilinear form on an irreducible unitary representation is a multiple of the inner product. For $F$ archimedean, Kirillov's conjecture is Baruch, *A proof of Kirillov's conjecture*, Ann. of Math. 158 (2003), and the irreducibility of products of unitary representations is part (1) of the theorem above. The statement is the field `bernsteinUnitaryProductIrreducible` of the [third place hypotheses](/step/unitary-group-third-place-hypotheses-def/).

**Example ($\mathrm{GL}_2(\mathbb{R})$).** The irreducible unitary representations of $\mathrm{GL}_2(\mathbb{R})$ are: the characters $\chi \circ \det$ ($= u(\chi, 2)$); $D_k \otimes |\det|^{it}$, $k \ge 2$; the unitary principal series $\chi_1 \times \chi_2$ for unitary characters $\chi_i$ of $\mathbb{R}^\times$; and the complementary series $\chi \nu^\alpha \times \chi \nu^{-\alpha}$, $0 < \alpha < \frac{1}{2}$. Restricted to $\mathrm{SL}_2(\mathbb{R})$ this is Bargmann's list; $\mathrm{sgn} \times 1$ restricts to $I^-(0) = D_1^+ \oplus D_1^-$.

**Corollary (generic unitary dual).** Let $F$ be nonarchimedean, and let $\pi$ be an irreducible generic unitary representation of $\mathrm{GL}_n(F)$. Then
$$\pi \cong \delta_1 \nu^{\alpha_1} \times \dots \times \delta_r \nu^{\alpha_r}$$
with $\delta_i \in \mathcal{D}^u(F)$ and $-\frac{1}{2} < \alpha_i < \frac{1}{2}$, where the $\alpha_i \ne 0$ come in pairs $\pm\alpha$ with the same $\delta$.

*Proof.* Write $\pi$ as a product of elements of $\mathcal{B}(F)$ by the theorem. The product is irreducible, and in the [Zelevinsky classification](/learn/representations/#generic-representations-and-local-factors) it is $L(\mathfrak{a})$ for the union $\mathfrak{a}$ of the multisegments of the factors. It is generic if and only if no two segments of $\mathfrak{a}$ are linked. The segments of $u(\delta, m)$ are those of $\delta$ shifted by $\nu^{(m-1)/2}, \nu^{(m-3)/2}, \dots$, and two consecutive ones are linked, so each factor has $m = 1$. $\square$

For $F$ archimedean the same description holds; it follows from Vogan's theorem and the fact that a Langlands quotient of $\mathrm{GL}_n(F)$ is generic only when it is the whole standard module, which we do not prove here. This is the field `genericUnitaryDualGL` of the [third place hypotheses](/step/unitary-group-third-place-hypotheses-def/).

**Remark (the trivial bound toward Ramanujan).** A cuspidal automorphic representation of $\mathrm{GL}_n$ over a number field has generic unitary local components. So the corollary bounds the exponents of each local component by $|\operatorname{Re} s| < \frac{1}{2}$: the Satake parameters $a$ at an unramified place satisfy $q^{-1/2} < |a| < q^{1/2}$. This is the bound of Jacquet and Shalika, *On Euler products and the classification of automorphic representations I*, Amer. J. Math. 103 (1981). Luo, Rudnick and Sarnak, *On the generalized Ramanujan conjecture for GL(n)*, Proc. Sympos. Pure Math. 66 (1999), improve $\frac{1}{2}$ to $\frac{1}{2} - \frac{1}{n^2 + 1}$. The Ramanujan conjecture asks for $0$, that is, for tempered local components; for the regular algebraic polarized representations of the [Galois row](/proof/galois-row/) it is known at the unramified places, through their Galois representations and the Weil conjectures.
